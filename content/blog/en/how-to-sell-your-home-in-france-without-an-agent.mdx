You can sell a home in France without an estate agent: private sales are allowed, and what you give up is the agent's work, not any legal step. You prepare the diagnostics, advertise, show the property and negotiate yourself, then a *notaire* formalises the sale. This guide follows that order and flags what must be done properly, because several obligations fall on the seller whether or not an agent is involved.

## 1. Prepare the property and the diagnostics

Start with the paperwork, before you list. The seller must assemble a **technical diagnostic file** (*dossier de diagnostic technique*) and give it to the buyer at the promise of sale or the deed. Notaries advising private sellers list the usual diagnostics as energy performance, asbestos, lead, termites, gas and electricity; which of them apply depends on the property, so confirm the list with a certified diagnostician.

Two of them have rules worth knowing, according to the official public-service portal:

- **Energy performance diagnosis (DPE).** It must be done by a certified diagnostician at your initiative and be less than 10 years old on the date of the promise of sale or the deed. The portal also says DPEs completed between 1 January 2018 and 30 June 2021 became invalid on 1 January 2025 and must be redone before a sale.
- **Gas diagnosis.** It is required when the gas installation is more than 15 years old, and it must be less than 3 years old on the date of the promise or the deed.

The same portal says that for a single-owner house or building classified E, F or G on the DPE, an **energy audit** must also be given to the buyer at the promise or the deed. The rule has changed in recent years, so check the current wording on the portal when you sell.

## 2. Advertise: what your listing must show

If your property has a DPE, your advertisement must show the energy and climate classes (A to G) legibly and in colour, with the estimated annual energy costs and their reference year. For a property classed F or G the ad must also carry the mention "logement à consommation énergétique excessive", according to the public-service portal.

Beyond that, a good private listing gives exact surface area, price, what is included, and the building charges for an apartment. Price by comparing similar properties in your area, or ask a notaire or another professional for a valuation, as notaries advise private sellers. ## 4. Show the property and keep a file ready

Prepare one folder to hand to serious buyers: the diagnostics, the latest property tax notice (*taxe foncière*), and for an apartment the co-ownership documents and recent charges. A complete file shortens the time between offer and signature and reduces the chance the buyer asks for a lower price after finding a surprise.

## 5. Sign the preliminary contract

When you and the buyer agree, you sign a preliminary contract. The notaries' site describes the two forms:

- **Promise to sell**: only you, the seller, are committed. The buyer has an exclusive option, typically two to three months, and pays an immobilisation indemnity of 10% of the price, kept by you if the buyer does not go ahead.
- **Sales agreement (compromis de vente)**: both sides are committed. The buyer pays a security deposit, generally 5% to 10% of the price, which is deducted from the price at final signing.

The notaries advise that these documents be drafted carefully and, ideally, signed before a notaire. Doing it without professional drafting is the most common way a private sale goes wrong, so do not use a generic template found online.

## 6. Expect the buyer's 10-day cooling-off period

For a residential sale the buyer can reconsider for **10 days**, counted from the day after the contract is first delivered by hand or by registered letter. If the buyer withdraws in time, everything paid is refunded. Do not plan anything irreversible, such as buying your next home, before this period has ended.

The contract may also contain conditions precedent, for example that the buyer obtains a mortgage. If the bank refuses, the contract falls and the buyer is refunded.

## 7. Complete the sale with the notaire

The notaire verifies the documents, checks for mortgages, liens or easements, and publishes the authentic deed. At signing, the buyer pays the price and the acquisition costs. Those costs, often called notary fees, are normally paid by the buyer: the notaries' site gives around 7–8% of the price for older property and around 2–3% for new property, mostly made up of taxes. As a seller you do not usually pay them, but ask your notaire to confirm what is charged to you.

## 8. Taxes and admin after the sale

The notaries' guide for private sellers says that a capital gain on the sale may be taxable, except for a main residence or other exemptions. Whether your sale is taxed, and how much, depends on your situation and where you are tax resident. We have not stated rates because we did not verify them from the tax authority for this guide; ask the notaire or a tax adviser before you sign.

## When an agent still makes sense

Selling alone costs time: viewings, calls and negotiation. If you live abroad, cannot be in France for viewings, or your property has legal complications such as inheritance or co-ownership problems, a professional can be worth the commission. The point is not that an agent is wrong, only that the legal steps above are the same either way.

## Quick checklist

1. Order the diagnostics from a certified diagnostician: DPE, gas if the installation is over 15 years old, and any others that apply.
2. Check whether an energy audit is required for your property.
3. Put the energy class and annual energy costs in your ad.
4. Prepare a folder: diagnostics, property tax notice, building documents.
5. Have the compromis or promise drafted by a notaire.
6. Allow for the buyer's 10-day cooling-off period and any mortgage clause.
7. Ask the notaire and a tax adviser about capital gains before signing the deed.
