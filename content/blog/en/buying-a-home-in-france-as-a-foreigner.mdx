Foreigners can buy property in France on the same terms as French citizens: there is no ownership restriction based on nationality or residence. The process is led by a *notaire*, a public official who makes sure the sale is legal, and the main cost on top of the price is the so-called "notary fees", which are mostly taxes. This guide covers the order of the steps, what you sign, what it costs and what you owe once you own the home.

## 1. Decide what you are buying: old or new

The type of property changes your costs. The French notaries' official site puts the acquisition costs at around **7–8% of the price for an older property** and around **2–3% for a new one**. Keep that in mind when you compare listings, because two homes at the same asking price can have a different total cost.

Evalorio is a free listings portal: you contact owners directly, with no agency commission on the platform.

## 2. Check the property and its paperwork

Before you make an offer, ask the seller for the paperwork a notaire will need anyway. Sellers must provide technical reports (the *diagnostics*), which cover topics such as energy performance. Ask also for:

- the title to the property and any information on mortgages or easements on it (the notaire checks this later, but you should ask early);
- for an apartment, the building's co-ownership documents and recent charges;
- the most recent property tax notice (*taxe foncière*), which shows what the yearly cost has been.

For a closer look at what sellers must supply, see our guide on [selling a home in France without an agent](/blog/how-to-sell-your-home-in-france-without-an-agent).

## 3. Make an offer and sign the preliminary contract

Once the seller accepts your offer, the sale is formalised in a preliminary contract. There are two forms, and the notaries' site explains the difference:

- **Promise to sell** (*promesse de vente*): only the seller is committed to sell at the agreed price. The buyer gets an exclusive option, typically two to three months, to decide. The buyer pays an immobilisation indemnity of 10% of the price, which the seller keeps if the buyer does not go ahead.
- **Sales agreement** (*compromis de vente*): both sides are committed to complete. The buyer pays a security deposit, generally 5% to 10% of the price, which is deducted from the price at the final signing.

The notaire normally drafts either document. Ask what you must pay at signing besides the deposit, and get the amounts in writing.

## 4. Use the 10-day cooling-off period

For residential purchases, the buyer has **10 days to reconsider**. The period runs from the day after the contract is first delivered, by hand or by registered letter, and you withdraw by registered letter. If you withdraw in time, all the sums you paid are refunded. The notaries' site calls this period "incompressible", which means it cannot be shortened by agreement.

Use these days: finalise your financing, read the conditions, and ask your questions.

## 5. Conditions precedent: protect yourself if the loan is refused

A contract can include *conditions precedent* (*conditions suspensives*). The best known is a mortgage clause: if your bank refuses the loan, the contract is void and the buyer is refunded. Non-residents can obtain French mortgages, according to the notaires' guide for foreigners, but the amount and conditions are set by each bank, so ask for a written offer before you rely on the clause. We do not give financing advice, and we cannot promise that a lender will approve a loan.

## 6. Final deed at the notary

The sale is completed when you sign the **authentic deed** (*acte authentique*) at the notaire's office. Before that, the notaire checks the documents and looks for mortgages, liens or easements on the property. At signing the buyer pays the price and the acquisition costs, and the notaire publishes the deed so that you appear as the owner.

If you cannot travel, ask the notaire whether you can sign through a representative. We have not found a general rule on this in the official sources we used, so rely on your notaire's answer rather than on a blog.

## 7. What it costs: notary fees explained

The acquisition costs are called "notary fees" (*frais de notaire*), but the notaries' official site explains that they have three parts:

| Component | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Transfer taxes and registration fees | The largest part, paid to the State and to local authorities, and variable by location |
| Notary's emoluments | The professional fee, calculated on a bracketed scale set by regulation |
| Disbursements | Payments for third parties such as registries and surveyors |

The notary's own emoluments follow this scale on the notaries' site:

| Part of the price | Rate |
| --- | --- |
| Up to 6,500 euros | 3.945% |
| From 6,500 to 17,000 euros | 1.627% |
| From 17,000 to 60,000 euros | 1.085% |
| Above 60,000 euros | 0.814% |

The site adds that a rebate of up to 10% is allowed on the part of the price above 150,000 euros. The fees are paid by the buyer and, according to the notaries, mostly collected on behalf of the State.

Those percentages are the notaries' own overview figures. Ask your notaire for a written estimate for your property, since the transfer taxes depend on the location and on whether the home is old or new.

## 8. After you buy: taxes for owners living abroad

Owning a French home brings yearly taxes even if you live elsewhere. According to the French tax authority:

- **Taxe foncière** (property tax) is due by the owner on 1 January of the year, whether the owner lives in France or abroad.
- **Taxe d'habitation** is still due on second homes. If your tax home is not in France and you own a property there on 1 January, it counts as a second home for this tax.
- You pay these to the public finance office where the property is located, not to the non-resident tax service.
- Since 1 January 2023, owners of built property must declare the occupancy status of each property in their account on impots.gouv.fr, by 30 June.

If you rent the home out, you have additional income tax obligations. Because those depend on where you live and on a tax treaty, ask a tax adviser; we do not state rates here.

## Quick checklist

1. Decide whether you want an old or a new property, and budget the matching costs.
2. Ask the seller for the diagnostics, the property tax notice and, for an apartment, the building documents.
3. Know which preliminary contract you sign (promise to sell or compromis de vente) and the deposit that goes with it.
4. Use the 10-day cooling-off period, and keep the registered-letter proof if you withdraw.
5. Get a written mortgage condition if you need a loan.
6. Ask the notaire for a written estimate of acquisition costs.
7. Plan for taxe foncière and, for a second home, taxe d'habitation every year.
